# Player Psychologist — Round 1

## Finding 1 — No onboarding path for a mechanic that punishes first attempts with permanent, irreversible loss
**Problem:** The reentry sequence is the game's most complex and least forgiving system: stage (one-way, kills all thrust), wait through plasma (chute shreds if deployed early), read a visual cue, deploy chute, then gear — and the punishment for a mistake is losing the cargo you just spent a whole run collecting ("cargo damages one slot per ~3 s at 100% heat, hull only after all cargo"). The document contains no tutorial, no practice mode, no first-launch scaffolding, and no low-stakes way to learn this sequence. A new player's first two or three reentries will almost certainly destroy their haul while they are still learning the ordering of stage → plasma → chute → gear. Losing the reward of an entire play session to a rule you hadn't been taught yet is a classic rage-quit generator, and it happens in the first 20 minutes of play. The design pillar even names "Anxiety from atmospheric reentry" as the target feeling — but anxiety only works as a positive emotion once the player understands the system; before that it is just confusion followed by punishment.
**Source:** Section 2.2 (reentry rules, cargo-damages-first), Section 1 pillars; omission of any onboarding, tutorial, or first-hour design anywhere in the document.
**Severity:** BLOCKING

## Finding 2 — The most punishing decision in the game hangs on a single subtle color cue
**Problem:** The only stated signal for when the chute is safe is "the hull glow fades from white toward orange." A white-to-orange gradient on a small pixel-art ship is a low-contrast, single-channel cue, and it gates a binary catastrophic outcome (chute shreds vs. chute works). Players under stress do not reliably read gradual color shifts, and colorblind players (roughly 8% of male players) may not read this one at all. There is no audio cue, no HUD element, no speed readout mentioned — and the "reentry sensor" that might have served this role is explicitly listed in the cuts (4.3). Expect players to blame the game, not themselves, when the chute shreds: the input they acted on was ambiguous.
**Source:** Section 2.2 ("When the plasma clears — the hull glow fades from white toward orange — deploy chute"); Section 4.3 ("reentry sensor" cut).
**Severity:** MAJOR

## Finding 3 — Failure recovery is undefined; the economy can plausibly death-spiral into a quit
**Problem:** The loss conditions are "stranded with no fuel, or 0 HP" — but the document never says what happens next. Restart from scratch? Reload a save? Is there a save system at all? Worse, the economy has a visible spiral: every launch costs money ("Armstrong fills the tank, and repairs the hull/heat shield at a flat fee per %"), and a crash destroys the cargo that would have paid for it. A player who crashes twice in a row pays full repair costs with nothing sold — the document only claims "a lazy run still breaks even," which assumes the run lands intact. Nothing addresses the broke-player state: can you afford a launch after a bad crash, or are you softlocked into a slow-motion game over? For the target feeling of "fear of wasting cargo value" to be fun rather than despair-inducing, the floor under failure has to be defined, and it isn't.
**Source:** Section 1 (loss conditions), Section 2.5 (launch costs, break-even claim); omission of any failure-recovery, save, or minimum-guarantee rule.
**Severity:** MAJOR

## Finding 4 — The win condition stacks the game's three cruelest constraints on its longest run, with an ambiguous "damaged" outcome
**Problem:** Armstrong's satellite "roughly doubles the ship's mass, barely fits a maxed hold, and arrives damaged unless the touchdown is soft." Soft means under ~5 m/s — but chute descent speed grows with mass, and this is the heaviest possible haul from the highest possible altitude, meaning the hottest reentry and the latest, lowest chute window. So the finale demands the game's most precise landing under the game's worst landing conditions, at the end of the game's longest flight. Every failed attempt costs the full round trip plus repairs. And the document never says what "arrives damaged" means for the win: is a damaged satellite a failed win, a partial win, a reduced payout? If players must repeat the entire multi-minute satellite run until they thread a near-impossible landing, the endgame becomes a frustration cliff exactly where the player should be feeling mastery. The Playtester agent asks "Is the satellite run beatable at full upgrades?" — beatable once by a sweep harness is not the same as reasonably repeatable by a human on their third attempt.
**Source:** Sections 1, 2.3, 2.6 (satellite mass, chute-speed-vs-mass, 5 m/s threshold), Section 3; omission of what a damaged satellite means for the win state.
**Severity:** MAJOR

## Finding 5 — Nine total purchases with break-even floors risks a flat motivation curve in the mid-game
**Problem:** The entire progression is "3 parts × 3 tiers = 9 purchases," with parachute, heat shield, and jetpack fixed at mid-tier forever. The document gives no upgrade prices relative to haul values, so the pacing between purchases is unknowable — but the structure itself is thin: after the early game, a player between upgrades has nothing to buy, no intermediate rewards, no cosmetic or narrative drip, and runs that merely "break even" produce literally zero progress. Repetitive suborbital hauls with no visible progress between tier purchases is where session-two retention dies. The greed loop (one more piece?) is genuinely good moment-to-moment psychology, but the meta-loop above it is nine beats long with silence in between.
**Source:** Section 2.5 (9 purchases, fixed mid-tier equipment, break-even lazy runs); omission of any pacing targets, pricing, or secondary reward structure.
**Severity:** MINOR

## Round 2 — Cross-examination

### CONFLICTS

**vs. Adversarial QA, Finding 4 (cargo as ablative armor — "exploit").** QA frames pad-the-hold-with-junk as an exploit that "inverts the intended greed penalty" and implies it should be closed. I'll argue the opposite side: from the player's chair, sacrificial cargo is one of the only self-directed mitigation tools the design accidentally offers, and it's exactly the kind of clever, legible risk-management play that converts my Finding 1's "confusion followed by punishment" into a feeling of agency ("I lost the cheap panels, but I *chose* to protect the engine block"). Loss you planned for is motivating; loss you couldn't see coming is a quit. My recommendation conflicts with closing the loophole: specify burn order deliberately (e.g., player-orderable or last-stowed-first), surface it in the UI, and make sacrificial padding an intentional mechanic. QA is right that the *ambiguity* is a defect; QA is wrong, from my lens, that the behavior itself is one.

**vs. Systems Designer, Finding 3 (the "land safer" purchase doesn't exist).** The Systems Designer offers two acceptable resolutions: "either restore a landing-mitigation track or delete the 'land safer' promise and accept (and state) that landing difficulty only rises." From a player-psychology standpoint the second branch is not acceptable, and presenting it as an equal option undersells the problem. My Finding 4 shows the endgame stacks the game's worst landing conditions on its longest, most expensive run; if difficulty ratchets monotonically with *zero* purchasable relief, the player facing the satellite run has no lever but raw retry — and retry costs a full round trip plus repairs. Deleting the promise fixes the document's honesty but leaves the frustration cliff fully intact. Only one of those two branches produces a shippable player experience, and the document should be told so.

**vs. Feasibility Lead, Finding 1 (hand-tuning fallback "rescues the game").** The Feasibility Lead writes that the sweep-harness collapse "rescues the game but silently deletes Section 3's measurement methodology" — i.e., the game itself survives hand-tuning. I dispute "survives." The two most quit-prone moments I flagged (first-reentry cargo loss, and the sub-5 m/s satellite landing at doubled mass) live inside narrow numeric windows; a hand-tuned chute window that's 15% too tight turns my Finding 4 from "frustration cliff" into "finale most players never clear," and no human will hand-feel that threshold correctly across nine mass/upgrade combinations. "The game ships either way" (4.4) is a schedule truth and a player-experience falsehood. The harness isn't just the methodology centerpiece — it's the only thing standing between my MAJOR findings and BLOCKING ones.

### CONNECTIONS

**Adversarial QA Findings 2/3 × my Finding 1.** QA's undefined states (press B on the launch pad → fuel-but-no-thrust softlock; press F during descent; jetpack-dry while tethered) read like edge cases from a QA lens, but combine them with the total absence of onboarding and they become *mainline* first-session events: an untutored player's primary learning strategy is pressing every key to see what it does. The game's first hour is exactly when players will stage on the pad and EVA at the wrong time. Undefined-state softlock + no tutorial + no stated failure recovery (my Finding 3) = a silent uninstall in session one. Each lens alone rates these MAJOR; together they compound toward the front door of the game.

**Narrative Critic, Finding 1 (undefined climax payoff) × my Finding 4.** The Narrative Critic shows the win moment has no described ending, screen, or beat; I showed the win attempt is the most expensive, most repeatable-failure-prone loop in the game. Combined: the design asks for maximum retry tolerance at the exact moment it offers zero anticipated reward. Players endure brutal finale retry loops when they can visualize the payoff (the ending they've been promised); this game's finale currently pays off into a rules ambiguity. Retry motivation is an anticipation mechanism, and there is nothing to anticipate.

**Feasibility Lead, Finding 4 (UI/HUD unbudgeted) × my Finding 2.** The cheapest fixes for my ambiguous-chute-cue finding are HUD-side: a heat gauge, an audio sting, a speed readout. The Feasibility Lead shows the token budget already omits "the shop UI, HUD, menus" entirely, with zero rework margin. So the fix for my MAJOR readability problem sits in precisely the work the plan has not budgeted — which means it is the work most likely to be cut on a bad day. This raises the odds that the shipped game communicates its most punishing decision through a color gradient alone.

### REVISIONS

**Finding 3: upgrade MAJOR → BLOCKING.** Three lenses independently converged on the broke-player spiral (Systems Designer F1, Adversarial QA F5, mine), and QA's version sharpened it decisively: the broke-but-alive state is reachable, triggers *neither* listed loss condition, and has no exit. I originally framed this as a motivational floor problem ("despair-inducing"); QA's sequence shows it is a literal undefined terminal state a player can reach in two bad runs during their learning phase — which is when they are most likely to have two bad runs. An undefined dead-end reachable in the first hour is a blocker, not a tuning gap.

**Finding 5: upgrade MINOR → MAJOR.** I rated the thin nine-beat meta-loop MINOR on the assumption that pricing would be tuned to keep beats close together. The Systems Designer (F2) and Business Analyst (F4) establish that no pacing target, run count, or price curve exists *anywhere* — and the Business Analyst adds that all 9 purchases are mandatory (satellite "reachable and landable only near full upgrades"), so the mid-game grind length is a direct function of numbers nobody has bounded. A thin reward structure with unbounded spacing between rewards is a session-two retention risk, not a polish note.

**Findings 1, 2, 4: unchanged.** Finding 1 (BLOCKING) was strengthened, not weakened, by QA's undefined first-session states. Finding 2 stands, with the added feasibility-side concern noted above. Finding 4 stands as MAJOR pending the Systems Designer's related questions (does one 3-second heat tick destroy the single-object satellite?) — if the answer to that is yes, it too becomes blocking, but that's a design decision the document still owes us.
