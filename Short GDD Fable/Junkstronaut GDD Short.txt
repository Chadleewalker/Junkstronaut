Junkstronaut
Game Design Document — First Draft  |  Chad Walker — Multi-Agent AI for Game Development, Assignment #1   
1. Executive Summary
A Kessler cascade has shattered every satellite in orbit into valuable junk. Mr. Armstrong, owner of the junkyard, wants it back — starting with his own lost satellite, hanging at the very top of the sky. You fly a rocket built from scrap: launch, get out, tether debris by hand, haul it home, and sell it to Armstrong for upgrades. 2D pixel art, keyboard and mouse only, built in Godot.
Core loop: repair/refuel and upgrade → launch to a suborbital arc or orbit → EVA and collect junk → reenter and land → sell.
Win: retrieve Mr. Armstrong's satellite — the heaviest object in the game, parked at the top of the flight envelope, reachable and landable only near full upgrades. Loss: stranded with no fuel, or 0 HP (hard landing, or reentering too hot).
Pillars: #Thematic — Scrapyard Spaceflight, blue-collar handmade spacecraft. #Mechanical — Physics Extraction, 2D flight and loot hauling. #Feeling — Anxiety from atmospheric reentry and the fear of wasting cargo value.

2.2 Reentry: Staging, Heat & the Deploy Sequence
Above a speed threshold in the atmosphere, heat builds and drag pulls; the deeper and faster, the hotter. The heat shield sits behind the thruster and tank and is exposed only by staging, which jettisons them — one-way, no thrust after. Shape your descent while you still have an engine, then stage once the final arc is set. Plasma appears above a velocity threshold; a parachute deployed during plasma shreds. When the plasma clears — the hull glow fades from white toward orange — deploy chute, then gear. Key values: heat builds above ~half orbital speed in atmosphere; unstaged reentry has no shield; cargo damages one slot per ~3 s at 100% heat, hull only after all cargo; heat dissipates ~5 s below safe speed; mass slows deceleration, so a full hold runs hotter and opens the chute window later and lower.
2.3 Touchdown Quality
Landings are graded by vertical speed: soft under ~5 m/s; damage scales past that; no gear doubles it. Soft landings require deploying the chute early to shed enough speed. Chute descent speed grows with ship mass (≈ square root), so a greedy haul drifts toward the hard-landing line. An intact landing sells the haul wherever you come down.
2.4 The Hand Magnet & Towing
A salvaged electromagnet on a winch spool, clipped to the suit. Fire it at junk within ~1/8 screen; it clangs on and winds the piece to a short leash that trails behind you. One tether at a time. The piece adds its mass to yours: jetpack acceleration scales inversely with total mass. Release is instant and clean (power off, piece keeps its drift). Stow at the hold (E) to bank it. The greed decision: every trip out costs jetpack fuel and every stowed piece is permanent mass for the ride down.
2.5 Fuel, Economics & Upgrades
Every launch costs money: Armstrong fills the tank, and repairs the hull/heat shield at a flat fee per %. Launch cost ≈ the value of 2–3 cheap pieces, so a lazy run still breaks even. Upgrades (3 parts × 3 tiers = 9 purchases): Fuel tank (capacity), Thruster (thrust), Storage (cargo slots). Storage is double-edged — a bigger hold raises the profit ceiling and the difficulty of the flight home. Parachute, heat shield, and jetpack ship at fixed mid-tier values.
2.6 Junk Value & Altitude
One suborbital band with a value gradient: cheap scrap panels low, denser and more valuable wreckage near the top of the envelope. Value scales with mass, so the good stuff fights you on the tether and on the way down — difficulty scales with success automatically. At the very top hangs Armstrong's satellite: it roughly doubles the ship's mass, barely fits a maxed hold, and arrives damaged unless the touchdown is soft. 
3. AI Architecture (Coder → QA → Playtester)
Junkstronaut's difficulty emerges from a single closed feedback loop: cargo mass raises reentry heat, heat delays safe parachute deployment, later deployment increases touchdown speed, touchdown speed destroys cargo, and cargo value funds the upgrades that raise carrying capacity. The loop is coupled and nonlinear, so its behavior is determined empirically by sampling the parameter space. The three agents implement that measurement. 
Coder: Implements one mechanic per session as GDScript. Every tunable lives in a single config/game_params.tres; hardcoded literals are a rejection condition, so any later session can understand a number from one small file instead of from the codebase.
QA: Writes a GUT regression suite from the spec and holds the ticket closed until it passes. Roughly ten invariants: staging is one-way and kills all thrust; the chute shreds during plasma; cargo damages before hull; towed mass scales acceleration inversely; no-gear landings double damage. A red test returns to the Coder with the failing assertion as the bug report.
Playtester: Runs headless Godot across a grid of config values and reports what the player would experience. Enabled by the deterministic fixed-timestep integration and the single config file. Can a full-hold ship reach the top of the band, reenter, and land under 5 m/s? Does the economy let a lazy run break even? Is the satellite run beatable at full upgrades? Each report proposes one candidate value set. The sweep harness is built once and run at two moments: the reentry sweep and the economy sweep.
4. Technical Strategy
4.1 Stack
Godot 4.x (2D), GDScript. Custom fixed-timestep gravity integration on CharacterBody2D (deterministic). GUT for tests, run headless. One config resource for all tunables. Claude Code subscription; Git. Art: pixel packs + Deep-Fold planet generator; Aseprite for gap sprites only.
4.2 Token Budget
 Claude Pro, seven days, Sonnet as the default model with Opus reserved for the two hardest problems (the fixed-timestep integrator and the heat/drag coupling). 
Planned cadence: Coder sessions target 40–60k tokens — one mechanic, its config entries, and the two or three files it touches. QA sessions run ~25k, since the test suite reads the spec and the invariant list rather than the implementation. Playtester sessions run ~30k, because the sweep executes outside the model context and only the outcome table returns. That is roughly 150–200k tokens per day, ~1.2M across the week.
4.3 Cut From the Scope (and why)
* Ablative shield & multi-pass aerobraking. The most tuning-hungry system in the design. Reentry is one committed pass against a simple heat bar.
* Multi-tether towing & shear. One tether, mass-only physics — the trips-vs-greed decision survives in coarser form.
* Size classes, fragile flag, compactor, crane magnet, oversized junk. All exist to serve the endgame. Every piece is one slot; the satellite is simply very heavy.
* Tow fee. An intact landing sells anywhere; crashing is the only penalty.
* Researcher and Art Director agents; multi-tether tech demo; reentry sensor; controller support
4.4 Risks
* The sweep harness. It is the one piece of pure infrastructure in the week. Timebox: half a day. Fallback is hand-tuning for the rest of the build — the game ships either way.
* Reentry tuning. Sweep 1 exists to compress this: the grid answers survivability, I only tune feel. Fallback: pre-agreed constants — fixed heat rate per unit overspeed, fixed plasma threshold — tuned only by moving the safe-speed line. One knob.
* Towing feels sluggish rather than weighty. Day-3 gate decides; fallback is rigid attachment (piece snaps to the suit, contributes mass only).
